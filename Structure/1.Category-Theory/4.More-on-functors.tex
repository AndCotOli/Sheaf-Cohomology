\chapter{More on functors}

In this chapter we introduce one of the most fundamental results in category theory: the Yoneda lemma.
We also define what an adjoint pair of functions is and state its relation with limits and colimits.

The main references for this chapter are \cite{riehlCategoryTheoryContext, maclaneCategoriesWorkingMathematician1978, aluffiAlgebraChapter02009}.

\section{The Yoneda lemma}

A common theme in these part of the thesis has been stressing that we want to study objects not by themselves but through its relation with their environment.
In particular we can apply these way of thought to \hyperref[def:category]{categories} themselves using \hyperref[def:functor]{functors}, and to \hyperref[def:functor]{functors} using \hyperref[def:natural-transformation]{natural transformations}.

Note that every \hyperref[def:category]{category} \(\category{C}\) has a \hyperref[def:functor]{functor} of the form \(\category{C} \to \Set\): \hyperref[ex:hom-functor]{the (covariant) Hom functor} that works by fixing an object \(X \in \category{C}\) and sending \(Y \in \category{C}\) to its set of morphisms \(\Hom(X, Y) \in \Set\).

Therefore we could try to study how a \hyperref[def:functor]{functor} \(F\colon \category{C} \to \Set\) works on an object \(X \in \category{C}\) by studying its relation to \(\Hom(X, -)\).
This was done by Yoneda and Grothendieck independently and it's what is known as the Yoneda lemma.

Instead of giving its proof, we will give an intuitive explanation the philosophy behind it.
But first, here is the statement.
\begin{theorem}[{Yoneda - \cite[Theorem 2.2.4]{riehlCategoryTheoryContext}}]\label{thm:yoneda-lemma}
  Let \(\category{C}\) be a \hyperref[def:category]{category}, \(X \in \category{C}\) an object, and \(F\colon \category{C} \to \Set\) a \hyperref[def:functor]{functor}. There is a bijection:
  \begin{equation*}
    \Phi: \Hom(\Hom(X, -), F) \isoarrow F(X)
  \end{equation*}
  given by \(\alpha \mapsto \alpha_{X}(\id_{X})\). This bijection is natural in both \(X\) and \(F\).
\end{theorem}

Let's exemplify how this works using the following situation: suppose you are playing a game with a friend that consists on guessing a card he is thinking from a poker deck.
Since randomly guessing is boring, you can ask any yes or no question about the card to guide your response.
Therefore you could start asking ``is the card red?'' or ``is the number bigger than 5?'' until you are only left with one possible option.
However, what if you were prohibitted from asking direct questions about the card and instead only could ask about its relation with other cards?
In this case there is also a winning strategy, for example instead of asking if the card is red you may ask ``is the card color the same as the king of hearts?''.
Therefore a card is completely determined by its relation to the other cards.

This is the philosophy behind \hyperref[thm:yoneda-lemma]{Yonneda's lemma}: objects are completely determined by its environment.

\section{Adjunctions}

Throughout mathematics several constructions appear that are ``approximate inverses'' of each other.
Probably the most recurrent is that of ``free'' objects constructed from a base space: for example the free group generated by a set is the group consisting of all of the words formed with the elements of the set and their formal inverses.
Another example is that of giving a topology to a set by considering the discrete topology.
These are both examples of a general phenomenon called adjunction.
\begin{definition}[Adjoint functors]\label{def:adjoint-functors}
  Let \(L\colon \category{C} \to \category{D}\) and \(R\colon \category{D} \to \category{C}\) be a pair of \hyperref[def:functor]{functors}.
  We say that \((L, R)\) is a pair of \textbf{adjoint functors} or that \(L\) is left-adjoint to \(R\) and \(R\) is right-adjoint to \(L\) if there exists a \hyperref[def:natural-transformation]{natural isomorphism} of \hyperref[ex:bifunctor]{bifunctors}
  \begin{equation}\label{eq:adjunction-hom-iso}
    \Hom_{\category{D}}(L(-), -) \cong \Hom_{\category{C}}(-, R(-))
  \end{equation}

  We represent this diagramatically using
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzcd}
      \category{C} \arrow[r, "L"{name=U}, bend left=25] & \category{D} \arrow[l, "R"{name=D}, bend left=25]
      \arrow[phantom, from=U, to=D, "\perp"]
    \end{tikzcd}
  \end{center}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}[Unit and counit of an adjunction]\label{def:unit-counit}
  Let \((L, R)\) be a pair of \hyperref[def:adjoint-functors]{adjoint functors}.
  The \hyperref[def:natural-transformation]{natural isomorphism} \eqref{eq:adjunction-hom-iso} gives the \hyperref[def:natural-transformation]{natural isomorphisms}
  \begin{align*}
    \Hom_{\category{D}}(L(-), L(-)) &\cong \Hom_{\category{C}}(-, (G \circ L)(-)) \\
    \Hom_{\category{D}}((L \circ R)(-), -) &\cong \Hom_{\category{C}}(R(-), R(-))
  \end{align*}
  In particular we get \hyperref[def:natural-transformation]{natural transformations}
  \begin{align*}
    \eta\colon \id_{\category{C}} \Rightarrow R \circ L \qquad \varepsilon\colon L \circ R \Rightarrow \id_{\category{D}}
  \end{align*}
  that satisfy the \textit{triangular identities}
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzcd}
      L \arrow[r, Rightarrow, "L\eta"] \arrow[dr, Rightarrow, "\id_{F}"'] & L \circ R \circ L \arrow[d, Rightarrow, "\varepsilon F"] \\
      & L
    \end{tikzcd}
    \begin{tikzcd}
      R \arrow[r, Rightarrow, "\eta R"] \arrow[dr, Rightarrow, "\id_{R}"'] & R \circ L \circ R \arrow[d, Rightarrow, "F\varepsilon"] \\
      & R
    \end{tikzcd}
  \end{center}
  
  Conversely, if \(L, R\) are two pair of \hyperref[def:functor]{functors} and \(\varepsilon, \eta\) are \hyperref[def:natural-transformation]{natural transformations} as above then \((L, R)\) form an \hyperref[def:adjoint-functors]{adjoint pair}.
  For a proof of this see \cite[Proposition 4.2.6]{riehlCategoryTheoryContext}.
\end{remark}

\begin{remark}
  \hyperref[def:adjoint-functors]{Left and right adjoints} are dual concepts.
\end{remark}

Just as we can speak of \textit{the} \hyperref[def:initial-terminal-object]{initial} and \hyperref[def:initial-terminal-object]{terminal} object in a \hyperref[def:category]{category} we can speak of \textit{the} \hyperref[def:adjoint-functors]{adjoint} of a \hyperref[def:functor]{functor}, this time up to \hyperref[def:natural-transformation]{natural isomorphism}.

\begin{proposition}[Adjoints are unique]\label{prop:uniqueness-adjoints}
  The \hyperref[def:adjoint-functors]{right adjoint} (resp. \hyperref[def:adjoint-functors]{left adjoint}) to a \hyperref[def:functor]{functor}, provided it exists, is unique up to \hyperref[def:natural-transformation]{natural isomorphism}
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
  Let \(L\colon \category{C} \to \category{D}\) be a \hyperref[def:functor]{functor}.
  
  Let \(R_{1}, R_{2}\colon \category{D} \to \category{C}\) be two \hyperref[def:functor]{functors} which are \hyperref[def:adjoint-functors]{right adjoint} to \(L\).
  This means that for all \(X \in \category{C}\) and \(Y \in \category{D}\) we have bijections
  \begin{align*}
    \phi_{X, Y}\colon& \Hom_{\category{D}}(L(X), Y) \isoarrow \Hom_{\category{C}}(X, R_{1}(Y)) \\
    \psi_{X, Y}\colon& \Hom_{\category{D}}(L(X), Y) \isoarrow \Hom_{\category{C}}(X, R_{2}(Y))
  \end{align*}
  Using this we can define a bijection
  \begin{equation*}
    \Theta_{X, Y} = \psi_{X, Y} \circ \phi_{X, Y}^{-1}\colon \Hom_{\category{C}}(X, R_{1}(Y)) \isoarrow \Hom_{\category{C}}(X, R_{2}(Y))
  \end{equation*}
  Since \(\psi\) and \(\phi\) are natural in \(X\) so is \(\Theta\).
  Therefore for a fixed \(Y \in \category{D}\) we have a \hyperref[def:natural-transformation]{natural isomorphism}
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzcd}[column sep=huge]
      \category{C} \arrow[r, bend left=50, "{\Hom_{\category{C}}(-, R_{1}(Y))}" ""{name=source, below}]
      \arrow[r, bend right=50, "{\Hom_{\category{C}}(-, R_{2}(Y))}"' ""{name=target, above}]
      & \category{Set}
      \arrow[Rightarrow, from=source, to=target, "\Theta_{-, Y}"]
    \end{tikzcd}
  \end{center}

  By the \hyperref[thm:yoneda-lemma]{Yoneda lemma} \(\Theta_{-, Y}\) induces a unique \hyperref[def:categorical-isomorphism]{isomorphism} in \(\category{C}\)
  \begin{equation*}
    \alpha_{Y}\colon R_{1}(Y) \isoarrow R_{2}(Y)
  \end{equation*}
  which ensemble into a \hyperref[def:natural-transformation]{natural isomorphism}
  \begin{equation*}
    \alpha\colon R_{1} \Rightarrow R_{2} \qedhere
  \end{equation*}
\end{proof}

We end the chapter by stating relation between \hyperref[def:adjoint-functors]{adjoint functors} and \hyperref[def:categorical-limit]{(co)limits} that will prove really useful later on.
\begin{theorem}[{\cite[Theorem 4.5.2]{riehlCategoryTheoryContext}}]\label{thm:adjoints-preserve-limits}
  \hyperref[def:adjoint-functors]{Right adjoints} preserve all \hyperref[def:categorical-limit]{limits} that exist in their codomain.
  
  Dually \hyperref[def:adjoint-functors]{left adjoints} preserve all \hyperref[def:categorical-colimit]{colimits} that exist in their domain.
\end{theorem}
